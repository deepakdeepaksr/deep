How we checked. Each vernier was read on an enlarged crop of its photo, with the printed main-scale figures fixing the

Limits. A vernier read from a photo carries about ±0.05 to 0.1 mm: in these photos no vernier line can be seen to meet a

3. Row spacing: what the readings mean
A footprint places socket centres. Across the outside of two pins a caliper reads the centre distance plus one pin width; across
the inside, the centre distance minus one pin width. The average of an outside and an inside reading taken on the same two
pins cancels the pin width exactly, and half their difference gives the pin width, which is a built-in check: for standard square
header pins it should come out at about 0.64 mm.

What 0.2 mm means. A socket contact accepts a pin a little off centre, but as a working figure a pin more than about 0.2 mm
from its socket centre needs force or bends. A 0.2 mm spacing error alone splits as about 0.1 mm per row. Added to the row
offsets of section 4, the modem's worst pins come to about 0.3 mm. That is why A8 and A10 are blocking: neither error is large
on its own, both are cheap to remove, and forcing a board into misplaced sockets bends pins on a part nobody can rework in
the field.

5. Which way the modem board's pins point
On the ESP32 board the header plastic is on the back, the side with the microSD slot, and the pins stand out of that side: with
the board components-up they point down into J1 and J2, as the carrier assumes. The modem board is the other way round. In
both of your photos and in the datasheet's, most plainly the perspective view on its first page, the black header plastic sits on
the component side next to the A7672S and the long pins stand up from it, leaning outward in perspective as upright pins do
(Figure 2). The datasheet's photo of the underside shows no plastic there, only the solder joints and the SIM holder. With the
plastic on top, the long ends of a standard header point up and only the short ends, about 1.5 mm, come through underneath:
too short to seat in a socket.

J3 and J4 are drawn for the modem board components-up with its pins pointing down. If its pins stand up from the component
side, there are three ways forward, and the footprint depends on which one you choose:
• (a) the pins also protrude at least about 3 mm below the board, enough to seat in a socket: the footprint stands as drawn,
and the SIM holder faces the carrier;
• (b) the headers are refitted pins-down on every board: the footprint stands, at the cost of rework and a heat cycle on each
board, and the SIM holder faces the carrier;
• (c) the board goes in components-down: J3 and J4 must be mirrored (the PWR_KEY row moves to the right-hand socket);

The microSD conflict
The EdgeHax board wires its microSD slot to GPIO10 (chip select), GPIO11 (MOSI), GPIO12 (clock), GPIO13 (MISO) and GPIO14.

Control: what PWR_KEY, RESET and PWR_EN do
The datasheet says the three inputs are level-translated and let a microcontroller power the module on, reset it and switch the
modem board's power on and off. It does not say whether each is active high or low, what state it takes when the ESP32 is not
driving it, or how long a pulse it needs. Your photo and the vendor's both show a 2-pin jumper, J2, beside the PWR_KEY label
near the top-left corner, with a shunt fitted, and two pads marked PW_EN beside it. On boards of this kind such a jumper often
ties the power key so the module switches itself on when power is applied. If so, the carrier's PWRKEY line does nothing while
the shunt is fitted, and the module can only be switched off by a command or by cutting PWR_EN.
That matters for recovery. A modem that stops answering in the field has to come back without a visit, and the robust way, the
recovery H2 depends on, is this: firmware sends the power-off command (AT+CPOF), waits, then switches the board off and on
with PWR_EN, cutting PWR_EN directly only if the modem does not answer. For that, PWR_EN must work as assumed, must
not be overridden by a jumper, and must hold a defined state while the ESP32 is in reset, set by a resistor on the carrier rather
than by whatever the GPIO floats to. If J2 does give automatic power-on and PWR_EN does switch the board, the PWRKEY and
RESET lines can be dropped, which frees two GPIOs (section 9).
One precaution: VDD_IN_UART stays powered from 3V3_C while the modem board is off, so the level translator stays alive.
Depending on the translator, a UART line held high can feed the unpowered module through its input. Until the vendor
confirms the translator isolates, set TX2 low or as an input before cutting PWR_EN (A11).

9. A pin plan for the EdgeHax board
The v1.2 allocation (its Appendix B) was drawn for an Espressif DevKitC-1. Three facts about the EdgeHax board break it: the
microSD slot takes GPIO10 to GPIO14, where v1.2 kept the modem lines and proposed E22 M1 on GPIO12; the LEDs on
GPIO40, 41 and 42 load those pins, where v1.2 proposed RS485 receive on GPIO40 and a microSD chip select on GPIO42; and
the octal PSRAM still takes GPIO35 to 37. This section replaces that appendix (C13).
Rules the plan follows
• GPIO10 to GPIO14 belong to the on-board microSD slot, and nothing else goes on them.
• Inputs, and lines that must hold a defined level while the ESP32 resets, go on pins with nothing else attached: GPIO38, 39,
47 and 48, besides those already in use.
• The LED pins (GPIO40, 41, 42) take outputs that are low most of the time: E22 M1 (low in normal mode), the RS485 driver
enable (low except while sending) and the status LED. A line that idles high would keep an LED lit and waste a few milliamps
around the clock.
• GPIO47 and 48 take their I/O voltage from the module's flash supply: 3.3 V on N16R8 and on the N8R2, N8 and N16
modules, but 1.8 V on 1.8 V-flash variants.
• The event button must wake the ESP32 from deep sleep, so it needs an RTC-capable pin (GPIO0 to 21). All are taken except
GPIO3, a strapping pin that only matters when the JTAG-select eFuse is burned, which it is not by default. GPIO3 with a
pull-up will do.
• If a 32 kHz crystal is fitted on GPIO15 and 16, the E22's M0 and AUX lines move.

11. How to measure and test-fit
Measuring one board, about ten minutes
1
Hold the board with its pins pointing the way they will go into the sockets. Measure at the pin roots, where the pins leave the plastic, not
at the tips.
2
Outside: close the main jaws on the outer faces of pin 1 of each row, square across the rows. Read O. Repeat at the last pin (12 or 22).
3
Inside: open the small upper jaws between the inner faces of the same two pins, square across the rows and not tilted along them. Read
I. Repeat at the other end.
4
Work out C = (O + I) / 2 and w = (O - I) / 2. Keep the pair only if w is between 0.58 and 0.70 mm; otherwise retake it. The two ends should
give the same C within 0.05 mm, which also shows the rows are parallel.
5
Repeat on a second board of each type. Photograph every reading with the jaws in view.
6
Send a table: board, end, O, I, C, w.
Test-fit before ordering
1
Set J1 to J2 and J3 to J4 to the mean C, rounded to 0.05 mm, with the rows level (A8) and J3 and J4 drawn for the way the modem board
will actually be fitted (A9).
2
Plot F.Cu and Edge.Cuts to PDF at 1:1 and print at 100 percent, with no fit-to-page. Check the scale with a ruler across the 168 mm board
outline: within 0.3 mm.
3
Lay the print on foam board or thick card and press each board's pins through it, the way the board will be fitted.
4
Pass: every pin pierces its own pad, none bends, each board sits flat, and the modem board's holes line up with the standoff holes drawn
for A12.
5
Better still, if a 3D printer is to hand: a plate with 1.0 mm holes at the pad positions and real sockets pressed into it.
6
Photograph the result and send it with the readings.
