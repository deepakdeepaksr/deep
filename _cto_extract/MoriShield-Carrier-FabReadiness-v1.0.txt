1. Verdict
Carrier v3 is not ready to print. As drawn, it would fail in the field in ways that are cheap to fix now and expensive
after a build. A fabrication release has to settle three things beyond module fit: that the copper carries the power,
that the supplies hold under real loads, and that the board behaves electromagnetically on a pole in an open field.
This document checks all three on v3, then puts every open change from MS-CAR-REV-A-001 v1.2,
MS-CAR-REV-A-003 and this document into one list (section 6), and sets out what Rev B must show before Serigreen
releases it for printing (section 7).
Area
What the check found
Verdict
Copper widths
Every power track carries its real current with a few degrees of rise at most, and
clearances suit the board's voltages. Width is not a heating problem anywhere.
Adequate
Modem supply
About 120 mΩ of track, cable and contacts from the buck to the modem board (140
mΩ with contacts at their 10 mΩ maximum). In a GSM burst the model gives 4.74 V
with a perfect buck, 4.64 V with the buck 2 percent low, 4.61 V with aged contacts and
warm copper, and 4.51 V with both, against the 4.75 V the board needs. Extra
capacitance does not fix it.
Fails: B10, now P0
Decoupling
Twelve supply points have no capacitor within 25 mm along the copper, among them
the modem, SPS30, E22 and ESP32 supplies. The 3.3 V regulator's 22 µF output
capacitor is 9 mm away in a straight line but 108 mm along the copper.
Fix: B7 (now P1), B12, C7
Input chain
Copper is fine (about 18 mΩ). The TVS, buck, fuse and input capacitor ratings do not
suit a solar battery input.
Fix: B1 to B3, B8
Ground and EMC
29 ground links between the two layers, only 19 of them to the main bottom pour;
nearly two thirds of the board is more than 10 mm from one. ESD parts are marked
do-not-fit on the PCB but fitted in the schematic and BOM; there is no earth terminal,
no filter on the buck's input and no EMC test plan.
Fix: E1, C8, H1, E7, G4
Module fit
Four blocking items from the module-fit review: microSD pins, row alignment, the
modem board's pin direction, and measurement with a 1:1 test-fit.
Fix: A7 to A10
Fabrication files
DRC clean by the team's report. Fab notes, revision, X2 attributes, test netlist and BOM
part numbers missing.
Fix: F1, F2

2. What we checked
Check
Method
Loads and rail budget
Typical and worst-case current of every module and part on each rail, from datasheets and the node design.
Copper paths
The least-resistance copper path from each source to each load in the v3 board file, at 1 oz copper; widths
and heating checked against IPC-2221 and IPC-2152 practice.
Modem rail under bursts
A circuit model of the buck, tracks, capacitors, cable and a GSM burst, run for fourteen cases (Appendix A).
Decoupling
The nearest capacitor on the same net to every supply pin, along the copper and in a straight line.
Ground
Fill regions on each layer, every link between the layers (through-hole ground pads and vias) and the pour
pieces it reaches, and a distance map.
Clearance
The smallest gap between copper of different nets on each layer, from the board file, against IPC-2221B.
Protection
TVS, fuse, ESD parts and earthing against every external connector.
Files
The team's DRC and ERC reports, the README and the Gerber set, against our own checks of v3 (netlist
parity, connectivity, Gerbers against the board).

Every power track is thermally adequate at its real current: the worst case, the 0.5 mm neck on the 5 V trunk, rises about 4 to 7
°C at the RMS current of a GSM session. The 1.5 mm 12 V tracks would carry the full 5 A of F1 at about 30 °C of rise, so even a
fault below the fuse rating does not damage them; the fuse itself is oversized for a load of about 1.5 A (B3). The 12 V nets keep
at least 0.25 mm from other nets and 0.30 mm from ground, which meets IPC-2221B Table 6-1 up to 30 V even for uncoated
component terminations (0.25 mm), so the 22 to 25 V battery-disconnect case (B8) is covered; conformal coating (H4) adds
margin outdoors. The project's clearance rule is 0.20 mm (the README says 0.18 mm), the team's DRC passes against it, and
the smallest gap we found is 0.22 mm, between 5 V and logic nets. The issue on this board is resistance on the modem path
and the sharing of one long trunk by every 5 V load, not heating.

Three things follow. First, capacitance cannot carry a burst this long: 1000 µF supplying 1.65 A for 0.58 ms would fall by almost
1 V, so in practice a capacitor at J5 covers only the first tenth of a millisecond, after which the path resistance sets the level
(case 5 against case 1). Second, the cable and its contacts matter as much as the board: four JST contacts can reach 80 mΩ
once aged, more than the whole widened trunk. Third, the cable end and the source location together set the margin. With
the carrier end of the cable soldered (or on a low-resistance connector) and a short cable, a converter beside the modem set
to 5.10 V keeps 4.86 V at tolerance and ageing (case 13). The same cable change on the widened trunk, with the main buck at
5.10 V, gives only 4.78 V (case 10), before the ESP32, E22 and SPS30 take a further 0.01 to 0.04 V on the shared trunk. The
dedicated converter keeps about 110 mV of margin against about 30 mV, the other 5 V loads cannot pull it down, it takes the
1.8 A bursts off the shared trunk and out of the E22's supply pad, which helps EMC (section 4.3), and its enable pin gives the
modem a true hard power switch (A11). B10 is raised to P0 on this basis.

3.4 Input chain and thermal
J18 feeds F1 (5 A glass fuse in clips), D1 (SB560 series Schottky for reverse polarity), then D2 (SMBJ12CA) and C6 (100 µF, 25 V)
at the buck input. The copper is fine at about 18 mΩ. The issues are ratings, already in v1.2: D2 starts to conduct at battery
charge voltage (B1); the buck module is listed to 24 V while the direction for Rev B is 40 V parts (B2, B8); C6 is rated 25 V (B3);
the 5 A glass fuse is oversized and cannot recover in the field (B3). C6 reaches the rail through a 0.5 mm neck and a via, 27 mm
of copper from the buck input (B11).
Thermal margins at +70 °C inside the box: D1 dissipates about 0.7 W at the worst-case peak of 1.5 A and about 0.1 W typically,
comfortable for a DO-201AD part. U1 dissipates about 0.1 W at the real 3.3 V load. The README's case of "AMS1117 about 90
°C at 600 mA" would be about 1 W in a SOT-89, too much at +70 °C ambient; the 3.3 V rail carries no such load, and it should
stay that way (B5, H3). The buck module's derating at +70 °C is unknown (B2, H3).
3.5 Standing drain
The always-on lamps (about 4 mA), U1's quiescent current (about 5 mA), the ESP32 board's regulator, LEDs and USB-serial
chip, and the modem board when not switched off add up to tens of milliamps around the clock, against a node design that
assumes about 10 mW asleep. B5 (budget per mode, switched rails, low-quiescent regulator) remains the request; B10's
converter enable lets the modem be switched off completely between sessions.
4. EMC and EMI
4.1 Ground and return paths
The top ground pour is one piece (about 17,070 mm²). The bottom pour is one main piece (about 14,870 mm²) cut by long
bottom-layer tracks, chiefly the 220 mm 5 V trunk, plus nine smaller pieces, eight of them held by a single pad and one by two
(J1 pin 22 and J3 pin 2) (E1). The two layers are joined at only 29 points: 28 through-hole ground pads and one via (J2 pin 1 sits
in the antenna keep-out and touches neither pour). Only 19 of them reach the main bottom pour; the other 10 tie the top
pour to one of the small pieces. Figure 1 shows how far each part of the board is from the nearest link.

Figure 1. Distance from each point of v3 to the nearest ground link between the layers. Nearly two thirds of the board (64 percent) is more than 10
mm from one, 36 percent more than 15 mm and 16 percent more than 20 mm, up to 34 mm. Counting only the 19 links that reach the main
bottom pour, 78 percent is more than 10 mm from one and the farthest point is 53 mm away. The largest gaps lie between the ESP32 rows and
from there to the right-hand edge between the modem and the E22, down the right-hand edge, below the RTC, and in the top corners.
On a two-layer board the return current of every fast edge wants the copper directly under its track. Where the pour under a
track is cut and the layers are seldom joined, the return detours, the loop it encloses grows, and the board radiates more and
picks up more. Stitching vias on a grid of about 10 mm, closer around the radios and the buck, and long runs kept off the
bottom pour (or bridged across it), fix this cheaply (E1). The acceptance becomes measurable: no point of either ground pour

4.3 Switching and burst currents
Two currents dominate the board's interference. The buck's input current is pulsed at its switching frequency; its only
reservoir on the carrier is C6, 27 mm of copper away behind a neck and a via, and nothing filters the ripple before it leaves the
box on the 12 V lead, where the lead radiates it. A pi filter between the input protection and the converters (a ferrite bead
with 10 µF ceramic either side, plus C6 connected properly) keeps it on the board (E7, B11). The modem's 1.8 A bursts, every
4.6 ms, run the length of the 5 V trunk and through the E22's supply pad: the familiar 217 Hz buzz of GSM equipment, coupled
into the LoRa supply and the shared ground. B10's converter beside the modem takes the 1.8 A, 5 V bursts off the shared trunk
and the E22's pad; its 12 V input still draws bursts of about 1 A, so its 12 V feed needs its ground return alongside and input
capacitance beside the converter (B10). The E22's own transmit pulses want local decoupling (B12) and a ferrite on its supply
(E7).
4.4 Radios
Three radios share the box: Wi-Fi on the ESP32 module, LTE through the modem board's u.FL pigtail, and LoRa through the
E22's SMA at the right-hand edge. The Wi-Fi antenna needs the carrier clear beneath it to about y 170, on both layers, for
tracks, vias and pads as well as pour (E3). The LTE pigtail should run away from the ESP32 antenna and the analog lines to a
bulkhead connector with a surge arrestor (H1). LTE band 8 and GSM 900 (at up to 2 W) transmit from 880 MHz, 12 MHz above
the top of the LoRa band, and band 5, if the fitted module uses it, transmits at 824 to 849 MHz, 16 MHz below it. The two
antennas need as much separation as the enclosure allows, and LoRa traffic should avoid modem transmit bursts (C10).

6.1 New and raised requests
B7 · Capacitors beside the 3.3 V regulator (raised from P2)
P1
Where
U1 (AMS1117-3.3, SOT-89) at (145.5, 172.5); its output capacitor C2 (22 µF, radial) at (134.5, 172.5).
What we found
C2 is 9 mm from U1 in a straight line but 108 mm away along the copper (about 70 mΩ and 0.1 µH): the 3.3 V track runs
from U1 down to the sensor ports at the bottom edge and back up to C2. The only capacitor near U1's output is C8 (100
nF), 16 mm along the copper. On the input side the nearest capacitor along the copper is C3 (100 nF) at 88 mm; C5 (470
µF) is 91 mm away.
Why it matters
The AMS1117 relies on its output capacitor for loop stability; its datasheet names 22 µF solid tantalum on the output as
ensuring stability in all operating conditions, and C2 is a 22 µF radial part, presumably aluminium. Behind 0.1 µH of track,
which resonates with it near 100 kHz, above which U1 sees mainly C8, a 100 nF ceramic, it cannot be relied on, so the 3.3 V
rail that feeds every sensor may ring or oscillate, which shows up as noisy readings and I2C errors that are hard to trace.
What to change
Place the output capacitor the regulator's datasheet calls for within 5 mm of U1 pin 2, and 10 µF within 5 mm of U1 pin 3,
both measured along the copper, with their ground pads straight to the pour. If B5 replaces U1 with a low-quiescent
regulator, follow that part's capacitor rules instead.
Closed when
Layout, and a scope capture of the 3.3 V rail at U1 during I2C traffic and a modem burst: no sustained oscillation; ripple
and load-step transients under 50 mV peak to peak (20 MHz bandwidth).
B10 · A dedicated 5 V supply for the modem (raised from P1)
P0
Where
The modem board's 2-pin supply connector, fed today from the buck on J9 through 221 mm of track to J5 and a cable.
What we found
The modem board needs 4.75 to 5.25 V at its own connector. The path from the buck is about 72 mΩ of track, about 120
mΩ with the cable and four contacts (140 mΩ with contacts at their 10 mΩ maximum). In a GSM burst (about 1.8 A for
0.58 ms) our model puts the connector at 4.74 V with a perfect 5.00 V buck, 4.64 V with the buck 2 percent low, 4.61 V
once contacts age, and 4.51 V with both (section 3.3). Extra bulk capacitance at J5 gains almost nothing, because the burst
outlasts what a capacitor can carry. Widening the trunk and raising the set point pass when new but fail at tolerance with
four aged contacts (4.68 V); even with the carrier end soldered they keep only about 30 mV of margin (4.78 V), which the
other 5 V loads on the shared trunk cut to about 20 mV typically and can erase at their peaks, against about 110 mV for a
converter beside the modem.
Why it matters
Dips below 4.75 V during network attach or a GSM burst take the modem board outside its rated input, where resets are
the classic field failure, and the same bursts run through the E22's supply pad and across the board every 4.6 ms.
What to change
Give the modem its own 5 V converter beside J5, fed from the protected 12 V rail (input rated with B1, B2 and B8), rated
2.5 A or more, set to 5.10 V with an overall set-point accuracy of 2 percent or better (a reference of 1 percent or better
over temperature and 0.5 percent feedback resistors), so that it stays between 5.00 and 5.20 V, inside 5.25 V. Beside its
input: low-ESR bulk rated for the B8 maximum plus 10 µF ceramic, with its 12 V feed routed with the ground return
alongside. At its output: 22 µF ceramic and low-ESR bulk (for example 1000 µF, within the converter's rated maximum
output capacitance and checked for soft-start into it); the model gives the same result without the bulk, so it is insurance
against a slower converter loop. Keep the modem's cable as short as possible, with the carrier end soldered or on a
connector of low contact resistance. Its enable pin, driven by PWR_EN with a pull-down, can then be the modem's hard
power switch (A11). Locking the modem to LTE (AT+CNMP=38) roughly halves the burst current where LTE coverage is
reliable, but is not the fix.
Closed when
The G1 model of the new circuit, then a bench oscilloscope trace at the modem connector at the lowest input voltage,
through an LTE attach and with the modem forced onto GSM: never below 4.75 V, never above 5.25 V.

E7 · EMI filtering on the supply and cable entries
P1
Where
J18 to J9 (12 V into the buck); J6 pin 2 (E22 supply); the cabled I2C ports J11 to J14 and J17.
What we found
The buck's switching current returns to the 12 V lead through C6 alone, with no filter between the converter and the cable
that leaves the box. The E22 shares the 5 V rail with the buck's ripple and the modem's bursts. The cabled I2C lines go
straight from the ESP32 to the connectors.
Why it matters
Switching noise radiated from the 12 V lead and sensor cables raises the noise floor at the LoRa receiver and can fail an
emissions check; unfiltered cable entries also carry outside interference straight onto the logic.
What to change
A pi filter between the input protection and the converters (the main buck and B10's): ferrite bead rated 3 A or more, 10
µF ceramic either side, plus C6, with the filter's output impedance kept well below the converters' input impedance
(damped by C6's ESR or a resistor in series with a damping capacitor) so that it cannot make them oscillate. A ferrite bead
and 10 µF at the E22 supply after B12's bulk capacitor. Series resistors of about 47 Ω on each cabled SDA and SCL line at its
port, next to the C8 protection, so that the low level stays within the 0.4 V that request C6 sets.
Closed when
LoRa noise floor (RSSI with no signal) with the buck and modem running within 3 dB of its value with them off; ripple on
the 12 V lead measured and recorded (G4).
G4 · EMC test plan
P2
Where
The assembled unit in its enclosure, with cables at installed length.
What we found
There is no EMC test plan. C8 (ESD) and H1 (surge) each name a test; fast transients, emissions, and immunity of the radios
and analog readings to the unit's own bursts are not covered.
Why it matters
Interference problems found in the field cost site visits; those found before scale cost a layout change.
What to change
A written plan: ESD to IEC 61000-4-2 (C8), surge to IEC 61000-4-5 (H1), fast transients to IEC 61000-4-4 on the 12 V port, a
near-field and radiated pre-compliance scan against IEC 61000-6-3, LoRa link margin with the modem idle and transmitting
(C10), and analog readings during modem bursts (C12).
Closed when
The plan with the pilot units; results before units go beyond the pilot.

Appendix A. The modem rail model
A circuit model solved in the time domain (backward Euler, 1 µs steps, three burst periods after settling). It is a model, not a
measurement: it shows how the margin divides between the parts of the path, and which changes move it. G1 and the bench
capture in B10 close the question. A burst lasts about six time constants of the path (about 0.1 ms), so every minimum in Table
5 lies within 4 mV of the set point less 1.8 A times the total resistance from the buck's source to the modem, its 20 mΩ output
resistance included: in effect the result is a DC calculation, and the capacitor values hardly matter. Without the bulk capacitor
at the converter, case 13 is unchanged at 4.86 V.

100 µF output capacitance, ESR 30 mΩ
